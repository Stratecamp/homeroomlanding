Family operations

Starter systems for a house everybody helps run.

These four cover most of the work that quietly eats a household. What's below is what we actually do in a home with seven people, one dog, and people staying over more weekends than not. It's free and there's no email wall. Take what fits your family, skip the rest, change the parts we got wrong for your house.

PRINCIPLE 01Visible beats remembered

If a job only exists in your head, you ARE the system. Write it somewhere the family walks past and let the wall do the nagging for once.

PRINCIPLE 02Rhythms beat willpower

Pin the work to a moment that comes around anyway (Sunday at 5, the first of the month). A decision you never have to make twice is time you get back.

PRINCIPLE 03Ownership beats helping

Helping is lovely and it still means somebody had to ask. Hand over a whole job start to finish, and the asking goes away with it.

The laundry system

Laundry isn't hard. It's never done and never assigned, which is how it quietly becomes one person's whole personality. You don't need to wash more. You need every load to have a name on it and a time it lands in a drawer, so clean clothes stop camping in a basket for four days.

🏗️ The framework: one day, one person, one loop

Set it up once, then it mostly runs itself
1
Give every person or pair a laundry daySeven people, seven days. Nobody's clothes are competing, and "I have nothing to wear" turns into a personal planning problem instead of a household emergency at 7 a.m.
2
Whole-loop ownership, not stagesWash, dry, fold, put away, all the same person. Splitting the stages is where laundry goes to die. A half-owned load will sit in that dryer until Thursday and you know it.
3
Shared loads get a named owner tooTowels, sheets, and linens get their own day on the calendar like they're a person. Anything unassigned is invisible, and invisible means it's yours by default.
4
Folded means away, same dayOne rule, said kindly, every time: clean laundry doesn't sleep in a basket. This one rule fixed more of our laundry misery than anything else on this list.
5
One hamper per person, sorted at the sourceSort when the clothes come off, not on a Saturday mountain. Lights and darks bins if your crew can manage it. One bin if they can't yet, and that's completely fine.

🔁 The rhythm

DailyOne load, start to finish, by whoever owns the day. Started before school, away before bed.
WeeklyLinens day (ours is Saturday) + reset the laundry room: lost socks bin emptied, supplies checked.
MonthlyOutgrown check. One pass through the drawers. Too small goes straight into a donate bin, not "later."
SeasonallySwap the closets and clean the dryer vent (see the maintenance system, it's a fire safety item, not a chore).

👧 Who can do what

Ages 3–5Match socks, carry their own basket, put away underwear/pajamas in a low drawer.
Ages 6–9Sort by color, fold soft items, put away everything of their own. Loads the dryer with a stool.
Ages 10–13Runs a full machine cycle with supervision. Owns their own laundry day start to finish.
Ages 14+Fully independent, including linens and stain treatment. Can own a shared load week.

⚠️ Traps we fell into

  • The mega-Saturday. Six loads in one day means one person folding all six, and we both know who. Spread it across the week.
  • Refolding their work. A lumpy seven-year-old fold is a working system. Redo it in front of them once and they've learned you'll always redo it.
  • The giant clean pile on the couch. The second everyone's clothes touch, nobody owns any of it. Separate baskets, always.

The maintenance system

This is the most expensive invisible work in the house. Skipped upkeep doesn't stay skipped, it becomes an emergency, usually on a weekend. And every single one of these items lives in somebody's memory right up until the day it doesn't. So get it out of your memory, onto a calendar, with a name next to it.

🏗️ The framework: one master list, four cadences

Build this once; it protects you for years
1
Brain-dump every recurring itemHouse, cars, bodies, pets, paperwork. One sitting, one messy list, don't organize yet. You're just emptying your head onto paper.
2
Assign each item a cadenceMonthly, quarterly, twice a year, yearly. If you have no idea, guess. A wrong date beats no date every time.
3
Put it on the calendar with a name attached"Somebody should call the dentist" is not a system. "David books dentists the first week of August" is a system. The difference is a name and a date.
4
Book the next one before you leaveBook the next appointment while you're still standing at the checkout desk. That's six months of remembering, gone, for free.
5
Keep a vendor list beside the schedulePlumber, HVAC, dentist, vet, dryer vent people. Names, numbers, when they last came. Now anyone in the house can handle it without texting you at work.

🔁 The starter schedule

MonthlyHVAC filter · smoke/CO alarm test · pet meds (flea, tick, heartworm) · garbage disposal + drain check
QuarterlyCar: tire pressure, oil check, wiper fluid · water filters · deep-clean one zone of the house · test the emergency kit
Twice a yearDental cleanings (everyone) · HVAC service before summer + winter · gutters · smoke alarm batteries at daylight-saving changes
YearlyAnnual physicals + eye exams · dryer vent cleaning · car registration + inspection · insurance review · vet wellness visit · water heater flush · chimney if you burn

📅 The scheduling trick

AnchorTie annual items to a month you couldn't forget if you tried: birthdays, school start, tax time.
BatchBook all the kids' checkups in one call, consecutive slots, one trip. Seven people, one afternoon.
SplitDivide the master list between adults by domain, not by task. One owns vehicles + house, one owns health + pets.
Log itWrite the date done next to each item. Next year's schedule builds itself.

⚠️ The expensive ones

  • Dryer vents. Lint buildup genuinely starts house fires. This is a yearly job, not an optional one. Put it on the calendar before you close this tab.
  • Water heater and HVAC. Both fail dramatically and expensively, and both are cheap to maintain. Servicing costs a fraction of replacing.
  • Skipped checkups. Miss one dental cycle and the next visit is a filling instead of a cleaning. Prevention is the cheapest thing on your whole calendar.

The meals system

Somebody asks "what's for dinner" about 365 times a year, and in most houses the same person answers every single time, while also holding the allergies, the preferences, what's in the freezer, and when the store order closes. Planning it once a week is an hour. Answering it daily is a part time job you never applied for.

🏗️ The framework: theme nights + claimed slots

15 minutes on Sunday replaces seven daily decisions
1
Anchor two nights with permanent themesTaco Monday. Pizza Friday. Two decisions you never make again as long as you live. This alone cuts the planning in half the first week.
2
Post the week where everyone sees itFridge, tablet, whiteboard, whatever you've got. A visible dinner stops the question before it's asked, and an empty slot starts looking like an invitation.
3
Name a cook per night, not just a mealThis is the big one. Dinner needs an owner, not just a name. Cooks claim their nights on Sunday, so nobody is being volunteered by an exhausted parent at 4:40.
4
Write allergy + swap notes on the plan itself"Dairy free: use coconut milk." Four words on the plan, and suddenly your partner or your teenager can cook it or shop for it without checking with you first.
5
Give one night to a kidKid pick night buys you one guaranteed complaint-free dinner a week. Nobody boos the meal they chose. Even the siblings give it a pass.
6
The plan generates the listShop straight off the plan, sorted by aisle. No wandering the store trying to remember why you came.

🔁 The rhythm

SundayPlan + claim + list. Fifteen minutes, whole week sorted. Prep one thing that saves a weeknight (rice, dressing, browned meat).
DailyTonight's cook checks the plan in the morning, not at 5pm. Thaw what needs thawing. That's the whole daily job.
OngoingWhoever finishes it, adds it. Shared list on every phone. The oat-milk rule ends the mental inventory.
MonthlyPantry + freezer sweep. Plan one week around what you already own before shopping.

👩‍🍳 Who can cook what

Ages 3–5Wash produce, stir cold bowls, tear lettuce, set the table. They're on the crew.
Ages 6–9Measure, mix, assemble tacos/pizzas, own the salad. Runs kid-pick night with a co-pilot.
Ages 10–13Stovetop with supervision, follows a recipe start to finish. Owns one weeknight dinner.
Ages 14+Full solo dinner including planning and the grocery add. A real cook on the roster.

⚠️ Traps we fell into

  • Planning meals nobody claimed. A gorgeous plan with no cooks' names on it still lands back on you at 5 p.m.
  • Cooking for the wrong version of yourself. Plan for Tired Thursday You, not Sunday Afternoon You. Five easy dinners beat two impressive ones and three nights of panic pizza.
  • Keeping the allergy info in your head. As long as it's only in there, you're the only one who can safely cook or shop. Write the swaps down.

The activities system

This is where family calendars snap. Every yes a kid gives is a driving job a parent takes on, and it stacks up so quietly that one day you look around and nobody has eaten dinner at the same table in three weeks. So this system is about capacity first and logistics second.

🏗️ The framework: budget the capacity, then fill it

Decide the ceiling before the season, not during it
1
Set a per-kid limit, in writingOurs is one sport and one other thing per kid, per season. The exact number matters way less than having one at all. With no ceiling, every season fills right up to overflowing.
2
Protect nights before you commitPick your untouchable nights first, like family dinner and reset night. Anything that lands on those is a no, and the no isn't personal because you decided it back in August.
3
Count the true cost, not the practice timeFees, gear, drive time, and the siblings waiting in the car. A one hour practice is usually three hours of your family's actual evening.
4
One calendar, everyone's colorsPut it on the shared calendar the minute you register. Not in a text thread, not in a coach's email, not in your head.
5
Assign the driver at the Sunday resetEvery event gets a driver's name for the week ahead. Carpool trades happen Sunday, while there's still time to fix a conflict.
6
Gear lives in a launch zoneOne bin or hook per kid, packed the night before. Hunting for a cleat at 7:42 a.m. is the most preventable disaster in modern family life.

🔁 The rhythm

SeasonallyThe capacity conversation. Before signups: what's each kid choosing, what are we dropping, does the total fit the family's real week?
SundayPreview + assign drivers for the week. Surface every conflict on Sunday, when trades are still possible.
NightlyPack tomorrow's bag. 30 seconds at dinner: who has what tomorrow, is the gear in the launch zone?
End of seasonThe honest review. What did each kid love? What did we resent driving to? Drop something without guilt.

🎒 Kid-owned pieces

Ages 5–7Packs their own bag from a picture checklist. Carries it in and out themselves.
Ages 8–11Knows their own schedule for the week. Responsible for gear being in the launch zone by bedtime.
Ages 12–14Tracks their own practices on the shared calendar. Communicates schedule changes to the driver.
Ages 15+Manages their own commitments, coordinates rides, adds events to the family calendar directly.

⚠️ Traps we fell into

  • Saying yes one at a time. Every single activity sounds reasonable by itself. Four kids' worth of reasonable is a second job. Decide on the total.
  • Bleacher homework. A little kid doing worksheets in the stands three nights a week is paying for someone else's season. Count that cost.
  • Finishing a miserable season because you paid. That registration fee is already gone. Call it tuition on a lesson learned and let the kid out.
